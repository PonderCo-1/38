Sources

The count: 38 trillion bacteria, about 30 trillion human cells
  1. 1
    Revised Estimates for the Number of Human and Bacteria Cells in the BodySender, Fuchs & Milo. PLOS Biology, August 2016. Supports: about 38 trillion bacteria and 30 trillion human cells in a 70 kg reference man (20 to 30 years old); a ratio near 1:1 instead of 10:1; bacteria live mostly in the colon; low counts in the stomach and upper small intestine; about 0.9 × 10¹¹ bacteria per gram of stool; total bacterial mass about 0.2 kg versus older claims of 1 to 3 kg; red blood cells about 84% of human cells; the old figure traced to one 1970s back-of-the-envelope estimate; uncertainty of about 25%; the ratio varies between people and groups.
  2. 2
    Revised estimates for the number of human and bacteria cells in the body (preprint version)bioRxiv, January 2016. Supports: each bowel movement removes about a third of colon bacteria and may flip the ratio toward human cells; the ratio is expected to be about a third higher in women.

What the bacteria do
  1. 1
    Role of the gut microbiota in nutrition and healthValdes, Walter, Segal & Spector. The BMJ, June 2018. Supports: gut microbes ferment fibre we cannot digest into short-chain fatty acids; butyrate is the main energy source for colon cells; diet changes the microbiota within days; much evidence comes from animal models and observational links, which cannot prove cause and effect. Note: this review repeats the older "100 trillion" figure, which the count above revised.
  2. 2
    NIH Human Microbiome Project defines normal bacterial makeup of the bodyNational Human Genome Research Institute (NIH), June 2012. Supports: microbes live on the skin, in the gut and in the nose; they help digest food and make vitamins and anti-inflammatory compounds; nearly everyone carries disease-causing microbes that cause no illness in healthy people; antibiotics shift the microbiome; a newborn gets its first microbes at birth. Note: this release repeats the older 10:1 and 1 to 3% body-mass claims, which the video shows were later revised.

A note on wording

"38 trillion" is an estimate for a reference young man, not a head count, with an uncertainty of about a quarter; individuals vary more. "Bacteria" stands for all microbial cells, because fungi and archaea are far fewer; viruses are not cells and are not counted. "Hundreds of billions per teaspoon" is our conversion from the per-gram figure, and "roughly one apple" is our comparison for 200 g. The napkin, the Guesser and the Counter are illustrations: the original estimate was a short calculation in a paper, and the recount was done by a research team. Kitchens, seesaws and chef-hat bacteria are analogies. This video is information, not medical advice; if you take medication or have a health condition, talk to your doctor before changing your diet.
